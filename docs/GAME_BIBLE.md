# MiguelMiguel — Game Bible

**Status:** Gate 1 planning authority
**Format:** short psychological/philosophical visual novel with light profiling
**Target length:** 45–60 minutes; 8,000–10,000 words
**Primary build:** offline Windows desktop in Godot 4.7.1

## 1. One-sentence premise

In a functioning near-future city where an automated allocation system decides who is useful, Detective Elias Venn of Meridian’s Civic Investigations Division must distinguish an imprisoned rebel from his biological reconstruction—and discover that identifying the original body does not settle which consciousness should be allowed to live as Miguel Aranda.

## 2. Player promise

You will notice small details, decide what counts as evidence, and feel the weight of placing a human name below an automated decision. The game will give you a factual answer about biological continuity, but it will not tell you which person deserves the name in any final moral sense.

## 3. Scope

### Included

- Nine major scenes.
- Six meaningful interpretation/profile choices.
- One final report with four conclusions/endings.
- Static illustrated backgrounds and portraits.
- Narration, dialogue, internal monologue, music, ambience, effects, and restrained transitions.
- Profile overlay, dossier/evidence viewer, dialogue history, save/load, autosave, settings, text speed, auto-advance, skip-read-text, volume controls, reduced motion, scalable text, keyboard navigation, and practical controller navigation.
- Windows export first; optional web export later if it causes no material regression.

### Excluded

- Backend, accounts, cloud saves, database, multiplayer, analytics, telemetry, monetization, advertisements, achievements, LLMs, generative dialogue, procedural mysteries, voice acting, lip-sync, skeletal animation, 3D, combat, walking sections, relationship meters, visible morality scores, collectible lore, crafting, inventory puzzles, and large route trees.

## 4. Setting — Meridian

Meridian is dense, lit, and functional. Trains arrive. Shops open. Towers rise. People make jokes, eat on benches, gossip, work, wait, and ignore one another. Its cruelty is administrative rather than apocalyptic.

Automation created enormous wealth while removing much of the workforce. The Civic Allocation System—the **Measure**—assigns changing evaluations for employability, housing priority, medical priority, credit access, surveillance risk, and civic usefulness. Defenders call it neutral allocation under scarcity. Critics know that scarcity was politically shaped: owners kept the wealth while people displaced by automation were judged for lacking economic function.

The city avoids the word poverty. It prefers *low participation*, *low optimization potential*, and *risk-adjusted service*. Police receive generated summaries. Officials can follow a procedure without believing they are cruel. Elites buy handmade goods and “human service” as luxury experiences while everyone else navigates temporary credits, informal exchanges, and family networks.

Technology is advanced but legible: surveillance, predictive systems, biological reconstruction, and incomplete neural-memory modeling exist. The story shows their consequences through forms, queues, screens, and rooms rather than machinery lectures.

## 5. The Measure, legal designation, and the human signature

Miguel worked as a model auditor for the Measure. He discovered that the system was not only predicting instability; it deliberately lowered services in districts predicted to become politically expensive. The resulting deterioration then became evidence that the district deserved fewer services.

After Rafael Aranda died following a medical-priority denial, Miguel copied internal records and joined the decentralized resistance called the Uncounted. He became its most visible name because he understood the system and because the city preferred to blame one face.

The Measure already knows which body is continuous with the man born as Miguel. A microscopic reconstruction marker identifies Miguel B as synthetic with extremely high confidence. The marker is scientifically conclusive, but it is not legally self-executing: the law does not allow a laboratory result alone to create a person’s operative legal designation or authorize transfer.

Elias’s signed designation creates a **presumptive legal identity** for the immediate proceeding and authorizes the connected transfer: the designated human is processed as the citizen Miguel, while the other subject is transferred into the institutional reconstruction/property process. The presumption can be challenged, but it is the clean human authorization the institution wants beneath its automated result.

If Elias signs for B while contradicting the marker, the report is accepted provisionally by the intake system because a human reviewer has created the presumptive designation. A conflict review is automatically opened rather than silently accepting or instantly rejecting the report. During that delay, B receives the human-prosecution protections attached to the designation, while A’s transfer and prosecution status are held for review. Hale investigates Elias because the report conflicts with conclusive scientific evidence and may represent professional error, coercion, or deliberate falsification. The Continuation therefore changes treatment temporarily and materially, but it is not a magical permanent legal victory.

If Elias refuses to sign, a documented emergency review or panel override may apply the Measure’s result. The override exists to prevent a missing signature from stopping urgent custody and safety procedures, but it does not provide the clean, accountable human authorization the institution requested. Elias remains useful because his refusal is itself an auditable human event: the panel must record why the designated reviewer would not sign, and the institution must carry a visible gap between scientific result and human endorsement. Elias loses the power to delay the system indefinitely, but he cannot make his nonparticipation morally or administratively invisible.

## 6. Main cast

### Elias Venn — the examiner

- **Age:** 40
- **Role:** Human Review Detective, Meridian Civic Investigations
- **Surface:** quiet, observant, dry, fatigued, professionally polite
- **Internal condition:** depressed, morally exhausted, alienated from his work, ashamed of passivity
- **Concrete dependency:** his employed civic status protects his housing and treatment priority. He fears entering the low-utility category assigned to the people whose reports he signs.
- **Arc:** begins by confusing nonparticipation with innocence; ends knowing that signing, refusing, lying, and remaining silent are all choices with consequences
- **Writing rule:** begin with coffee, messages, forms, and case numbers—not philosophical speeches

### Miguel A — the biological original

- **Age:** 34
- **Legal status:** human citizen, accused rebel leader
- **Temperament:** intense, impatient, articulate, wounded, capable of tenderness and cruelty
- **Truth:** biologically and chronologically continuous with the man born as Miguel
- **Moral complication:** planned the records-facility attack after learning night workers were inside; accepted their likely deaths
- **Relationship to B:** hates that B is an instrument wearing his memories and resembles the person he once believed himself to be

### Miguel B — the reconstruction

- **Apparent age:** 34
- **Legal status:** institutional biological property
- **Temperament:** controlled, attentive, self-questioning, compassionate, sometimes unnervingly precise
- **Truth:** biological reconstruction made from stolen medical scans, surveillance, and a neural model; memories match through the reconstruction snapshot
- **Moral complication:** used an institution-controlled emergency channel to warn the workers, knowing the channel could expose the Uncounted’s access route. The workers survived, but authorities captured rebels and destroyed most of the leaked evidence. He chose immediate visible lives over the movement’s chance at uncertain future systemic change.
- **Relationship to A:** recognizes the same history but rejects A’s willingness to turn workers into acceptable losses

### Mara Hale — the procedure

- **Age:** 52
- **Role:** Senior Investigator and supervisor, Civic Investigations Division
- **Temperament:** calm, competent, respectful, procedurally precise
- **Belief:** systems prevent arbitrary human prejudice; imperfect allocation is preferable to collapse
- **Function:** represents procedural distance; never says “kill him,” only “complete the designation”

### Lena — the opening mirror

- **Age:** 31
- **Status:** unemployed former nurse’s aide
- **Fact:** sold access to part of her identity allowance to buy medicine for someone dependent on her
- **Function:** establishes that factual guilt and moral justice can diverge before Miguel appears
- **Voice:** plain, direct, refuses to perform helplessness

## 7. Story questions

The player should not hear these as a list at the beginning. They emerge from concrete situations:

- Is bodily continuity enough to establish personal identity?
- Does artificial origin invalidate sincere memory, pain, or moral judgment?
- If human choices are shaped by trauma and institutions, are they freer than programmed choices?
- Can someone remain the same person after abandoning values that once defined them?
- Can refusing to judge remain innocent when another procedure will act in your place?
- How do institutions use human approval to disguise automated decisions as morally legitimate?

## 8. Canonical facts

1. Miguel A is the biological and chronological original.
2. Miguel B is the biological reconstruction.
3. B inherits Miguel’s memories through the reconstruction snapshot.
4. B’s first independent memory is awakening inside the institution.
5. A planned to partially destroy the records archive knowing night workers remained inside and chose possible systemic change over those immediate lives.
6. B discovered the plan, knew the workers were present, used an institution-controlled emergency channel to warn them, and knew the channel could expose the Uncounted’s access route.
7. The workers escaped, but the warning disrupted rather than stopped the attack: the archive was partially destroyed, the Measure continued, authorities captured rebels, and most of the leaked evidence was destroyed. B chose immediate lives despite potentially preserving the system’s future harm.
8. Hale and the Measure know the biological result before Elias interviews the subjects.
9. The marker is scientifically conclusive but not legally self-executing; Elias’s signed designation creates a presumptive legal identity and authorizes transfer.
10. A conflicting signed designation triggers delayed conflict review; refusal triggers a documented emergency review or panel override.
11. No hidden file proves that B’s compassion is either authentic or fake.
12. No ending is the canonical moral answer.

## 9. Structure and player experience

The story uses a **branch-and-bottleneck** structure. In simple terms, some choices change wording and Elias’s profile, but the main investigation returns to shared scenes so the scope remains manageable. The final report is the largest consequence point.

The player sees six authored interpretation choices. Each one:

- uses neutral, defensible sentences;
- changes hidden profile axes;
- adds one visible dossier observation;
- can change later internal monologue or a final response;
- never changes history or reveals a correct answer.

At the end, the profile heading changes from `SUBJECT: MIGUEL` to `SUBJECT: ELIAS VENN`. It contains 4–6 authored observations and ends with:

> Would you accept being judged by the method you used?

## 10. Four reports

### The Original

Elias designates A according to biological evidence. His signed report creates the presumptive legal identity used for the immediate proceeding: A is prosecuted, B is scheduled for reclamation, and Elias remains employed. The report is factually correct while still leaving him uneasy about what the correct report authorizes. His housing and treatment priority remain tied to the civic employment status that made signing possible.

### The Continuation

Elias signs a designation in favor of B despite the marker. The institution initially honors the signed report as a presumptive legal identity: B receives the human-prosecution protections and A’s reclamation/transfer is held rather than immediately completed. The conflict review then compares the report with the marker. Hale investigates Elias for deliberate falsification or unfitness, and the review can overturn the presumption. This is not a simple rescue or instant permanent victory: Elias creates a real temporary change in treatment, but B remains inside a contested legal process. Elias transfers another person’s historical guilt onto B while accepting removal and investigation.

### Two Names

Elias files a noncompliant report recognizing both as psychologically continuous persons. It does not create a third automatic legal category: the report is rejected, the required human designation remains unresolved, and the institution moves to documented review while Elias is removed from the case. The immediate fate of both Miguels remains uncertain because the report has interrupted clean authorization without permanently defeating the legal machinery.

### No Signature

Elias refuses to sign. The institution records the refusal and convenes the documented emergency review or panel override. The panel applies the Measure’s known result: A is prosecuted and B is reclaimed. Elias loses the clean authorization the institution wanted, but the override prevents his refusal from stopping urgent procedure. He avoids authorship, not consequence; his employment and professional record now contain an auditable refusal.

## 11. Identity-neutral presentation rules

- Do not make calmness, anger, response speed, portrait treatment, color, or side a proof of identity.
- Amber and cyan may distinguish subject positions only; they never mean original/artificial.
- All clue-bearing gestures are described in text.
- Both Miguels receive the same visual capability set.
- The proof arrives from the synthesis marker, not a cinematic “tell.”

## 12. Beginner glossary

- **Visual novel:** a story game where text, portraits, backgrounds, choices, and audio carry most of the experience.
- **Scene:** a Godot screen or room definition. A future `Story` scene is the main screen where the dialogue is shown.
- **Node:** a single piece in a Godot scene, such as a button, label, image, or panel.
- **Autoload:** a shared Godot script that remains available across scenes, useful for saves or settings.
- **Story graph:** the connected set of dialogue and choice nodes. A validator checks that the player can reach every ending.
- **Save schema:** the list and types of values written to a save file, with a version number so future changes can be handled safely.
